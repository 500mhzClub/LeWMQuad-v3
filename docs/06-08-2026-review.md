# Literature review: what positive embodied JEPA results actually show

## Main conclusion

The literature does **not** support the simple explanation that sufficiently varied video automatically forces a JEPA encoder to preserve detailed geometry.

The strongest evidence is V-JEPA 2.1. Its predecessor, V-JEPA 2, had already been trained at enormous scale—roughly one to 1.6 million hours of video—yet its patch features still showed fragmented local spatial structure and weak linear-probe depth and segmentation. V-JEPA 2.1 obtained its largest initial dense-feature improvement by changing **where and how the predictive loss acts**, particularly by supervising visible context tokens and intermediate encoder layers. Larger and more varied datasets then improved the representation further. ([arXiv][1])

There is a second important correction to the usual description of the official robot results:

> **The V-JEPA 2 and V-JEPA 2.1 robot-planning stages did not primarily fine-tune the visual encoder on robot actions. They froze the pretrained video encoder and trained a large action-conditioned predictor on top.**

Consequently, their positive robot results show that large-scale video pretraining produced a representation containing enough spatial information for their control tasks. They do **not** show that robot-action prediction gradients can move an encoder while retaining geometry—the exact problem your recent experiments have exposed. ([arXiv][1])

Your thesis deliberately sets a higher standard than low latent prediction loss: the representation must retain geometry, semantics and controllability for navigation. Your current bottleneck is specifically transferable occupied-space information, not whether the encoder is nonconstant or can support generic frame prediction.  

## “Spatial information” covers several different capabilities

The literature often uses “spatial” rather loosely. It is useful to distinguish five levels:

1. **Image-plane locality:** object boundaries and semantic parts occupy coherent patch tokens.
2. **Relative geometry:** which object is left/right, near/far, or in front of another.
3. **Metric and dynamic geometry:** depth, ego-motion, object displacement and action effects.
4. **Navigation geometry:** visible free space, occupied space, occlusion and unknown space in a robot-centred coordinate system.
5. **Persistent spatial state:** topology, revisitation, place identity and information outside the current field of view.

V-JEPA 2.1 gives strong evidence for levels 1–3. DINO-WM gives evidence that dense patch features support some control tasks at levels 1–3. The official embodied papers provide much less evidence for level 4, and almost none for level 5. Your Go2 problem requires levels 3–5, with unusually strict emphasis on false-free predictions and occupied boundaries.

That distinction explains how a representation can succeed at cup grasping or visual servoing while still failing your occupancy qualification.

# 1. V-JEPA 2 and V-JEPA 2-AC

## Training recipe

V-JEPA 2 first learns an action-free video representation from internet-scale video using masked latent prediction. The official action-conditioned robot model, V-JEPA 2-AC, then:

* freezes the video encoder;
* retains a dense `16×16×1408` feature map for each frame;
* trains an approximately 300-million-parameter, 24-layer transformer predictor;
* conditions it on actions and the robot’s seven-dimensional end-effector state;
* uses teacher-forced next-feature prediction plus a two-step rollout objective;
* trains on less than 62 hours, or around 23,000 trajectories, from DROID. ([arXiv][1])

That is much closer to:

> **large pretrained spatial encoder + robot-specific latent dynamics**

than to:

> **jointly learning a robot representation and predictor from robot data.**

The V-JEPA encoder had moved during enormous action-free video pretraining, but it did not move during the core action-conditioned robot stage.

## What spatial information did it retain?

The robot results demonstrate meaningful spatial information. From an uncalibrated monocular camera, V-JEPA 2-AC moved the Franka end effector to within four centimetres of image-specified goals. Its latent planning energy was smooth and approximately minimized near the correct Cartesian action. That is credible evidence that the frozen representation retained relative depth, object location and action-relevant 3D structure. ([arXiv][1])

But the setting made that requirement more tractable than yours:

* a fixed exocentric table-top camera;
* an explicit seven-dimensional end-effector state;
* bounded actions of roughly 13 centimetres or less;
* blocking control;
* receding-horizon replanning after every executed action;
* image-goal matching rather than explicit free/occupied prediction. ([arXiv][1])

Closed-loop replanning can correct small geometric errors. A representation need not provide a globally consistent metric map to produce a useful local gradient toward an image goal.

## The crucial retrospective result

V-JEPA 2.1 later examined V-JEPA 2’s dense representations directly and found that they were **not especially good local geometric features**:

* feature maps were noisy and spatially fragmented;
* ADE20K semantic segmentation was only `22.2` mIoU;
* NYUv2 depth was `0.682` RMSE under a simple frozen-feature linear probe. ([arXiv][2])

So V-JEPA 2 retained enough coarse spatial structure for short closed-loop manipulation, but not the level of clean local structure one would infer from its robot success alone.

That is probably the closest published analogue to your concern: **a world model can control successfully without its representation satisfying a stringent dense-geometry qualification.**

# 2. V-JEPA 2.1: the most informative result for your spatial problem

V-JEPA 2.1 is the clearest answer to whether geometry came from data variety or from the learning objective.

## Why V-JEPA 2’s huge dataset was not enough

The authors’ diagnosis was that V-JEPA 2 applied its prediction loss only to masked target regions. Visible context tokens were input to the predictor, but were not themselves forced to retain locally grounded information. They could therefore behave partly as global aggregators rather than representing their own spatial locations faithfully. ([arXiv][2])

V-JEPA 2.1 introduced four changes:

* **dense predictive loss:** both visible and masked tokens contribute to the objective;
* **deep self-supervision:** predictive losses are applied at several intermediate encoder layers;
* **joint image/video training:** modality-specific tokenizers feed one encoder;
* **model, data and spatial-resolution scaling.** ([arXiv][2])

The first two changes are particularly relevant to your experience. They put direct pressure on every local token and on earlier layers to remain spatially meaningful.

## The ablation answers the “more data?” question

The progression was:

| Recipe                     | NYUv2 depth RMSE ↓ | ADE20K mIoU ↑ |
| -------------------------- | -----------------: | ------------: |
| V-JEPA 2                   |              0.682 |          22.2 |
| + visible-context loss     |              0.474 |          33.8 |
| + deep self-supervision    |              0.463 |          38.6 |
| + VisionMix data           |              0.418 |          40.8 |
| + multimodal tokenizer     |              0.415 |          41.4 |
| + model scaling            |              0.365 |          47.1 |
| + high-resolution cooldown |              0.307 |          47.9 |

The first objective change reduced depth error from `0.682` to `0.474` and raised segmentation from `22.2` to `33.8`. Expanding the data after the objective and deep-supervision changes improved depth from `0.463` to `0.418` and segmentation from `38.6` to `40.8`. Data clearly mattered, but it was **not the initial reason dense geometry emerged**. ([arXiv][2])

The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Diversity supplies the visual evidence from which geometry can be learned, but the objective must make preserving local geometry useful. Otherwise even internet-scale video can produce strong global semantics and weak local structure.**

Model capacity and input resolution also mattered. The high-resolution cooldown improved NYUv2 depth from `0.365` to `0.307`, which is relevant to your open-obstacle-field failures: a `112×112` image may simply contain too little source detail for small or isolated obstacles, even when it yields the same nominal `16×16` token grid as a larger-pixel encoder. ([arXiv][2])

## Did the improved dense features help robots?

Yes, but within a limited task domain.

V-JEPA 2.1 retained a frozen encoder and trained the same approximately 300-million-parameter action predictor on DROID. On the table-top Franka evaluation:

* reach remained `100%`;
* grasp improved from `60%` to `70%` at horizon one;
* grasp reached `80%` with a longer rollout;
* pick-and-place remained `80%`.

The authors attribute the grasp improvement to better depth and dense features. They also found that V-JEPA 2.1 could benefit from longer latent rollouts where V-JEPA 2 degraded. ([arXiv][2])

V-JEPA 2.1 also reports navigation results, but this is a **two-second open-loop trajectory-prediction task** evaluated with trajectory error. A conditional diffusion transformer is trained on top of the frozen representation, and CEM selects a trajectory in a reduced three-degree-of-freedom action space. It is not a closed-loop test of unknown/free/occupied perception, collision safety, maze completion or long-horizon partial observability. ([arXiv][2])

So V-JEPA 2.1 provides strong evidence that dense pretraining improves both geometry probes and some robot control. It does not yet demonstrate the complete spatial contract your navigation task requires.

# 3. DINO-WM: dense image features can outperform video JEPA features

DINO-WM is not trained with a JEPA encoder, but it is one of the most relevant JEPA-style world-model comparators. It freezes DINOv2 and trains an action-conditioned predictor over its dense patch tokens. It then performs goal-directed planning entirely in feature space. ([arXiv][3])

Its patch-versus-global ablation is unusually informative:

| Encoder representation | Maze | Wall | Reach | PushT |
| ---------------------- | ---: | ---: | ----: | ----: |
| DINO CLS/global        | 0.96 | 0.58 |  0.60 |  0.44 |
| DINO patch tokens      | 0.98 | 0.96 |  0.92 |  0.90 |

Global representations remained sufficient for the simple Maze task, but performance collapsed as precise object position, boundary and control information became important. The authors explicitly attribute the patch-token advantage to preserving spatial detail lost by global pooling. ([arXiv][3])

This strongly supports your decision to demote the learned BEV bottleneck and retain dense patch tokens as the primary representation. It also confirms that **representation topology matters independently of prediction loss**.

However, DINO-WM’s Maze and Wall tasks are simple, fully visible image-goal tasks, and its manipulation observations are generally much more direct than a moving first-person quadruped camera. It demonstrates that patch tokens can support planning; it does not demonstrate reliable egocentric occupancy estimation under occlusion. ([arXiv][3])

# 4. “What Drives Success in Physical Planning with JEPA-WMs?”

This recent systematic study is perhaps more useful for your immediate engineering choices than any single architecture paper.

All visual encoders in the study were frozen. The authors compared DINOv2, DINOv3, V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2 patch tokens under matched predictor and planning experiments. They found that the DINO image encoders generally outperformed the video encoders for their navigation and manipulation tasks. Their explanation is sharper object segmentation: separate objects occupy localized tokens with crisp boundaries, making motion appear as sparse, learnable token changes. Coarser V-JEPA features distribute object information across overlapping tokens, making action-conditioned dynamics harder to isolate. ([arXiv][4])

This rules out another tempting assumption:

> A video encoder is not automatically better for physical planning than an image encoder.

Video pretraining may produce stronger motion semantics, while dense image SSL may produce cleaner object boundaries. The best representation depends on what the predictor must resolve.

The same study found two further results directly relevant to your recent work:

* two frames are needed to infer velocity, while three frames can support acceleration;
* proprioception consistently improves performance because frozen visual patches represent coarse layout better than precise metric quantities such as end-effector coordinates. ([arXiv][4])

Their preferred simulated-task recipe uses:

* dense DINO patch tokens;
* three frames of context;
* proprioception;
* two-step rollout training;
* an AdaLN-conditioned transformer predictor;
* CEM planning. ([arXiv][4])

This is strong independent support for moving away from a single-image state. Your input-sufficiency diagnostic found only a modest improvement from three frames, but it used a very small matched-branch corpus. The broader literature says temporal context matters once the dataset is large and diverse enough for the model to generalize.

# 5. Genuine end-to-end JEPAs with encoder movement

## LeWorldModel

LeWorldModel is a real counterexample to the idea that the encoder must always be frozen. It jointly trains an encoder and action-conditioned predictor directly from pixels using:

[
L_{\mathrm{prediction}}+\lambda L_{\mathrm{SIGReg}}.
]

It reports successful latent planning on Two-Room, Push-T and OGBench-Cube, along with probes showing that physical quantities can be decoded from the representation. ([arXiv][5])

But its positive result does not show that the latent preserves high-quality dense navigation geometry. It uses a compact global representation, and its main control environments are relatively bounded and visually simple. SIGReg guarantees a broadly distributed representation, not that the variance is organized around occupied boundaries, metric distance or visibility.

That matches your empirical result: a representation can be noncollapsed and predictive while still failing to retain the spatial distinctions required for navigation. Your draft already recognizes that anti-collapse is not a substitute for semantic and physical qualification. 

## Depth-regularized JEPA: particularly relevant to your next decision

A July 2026 preprint extends LeWorldModel on real agricultural robot video using a training-only depth teacher. It uses:

* three-frame RGB history;
* actions and proprioception;
* a ViT-Tiny encoder;
* 192-dimensional latents;
* an end-to-end predictive objective plus SIGReg;
* RGB/depth representation alignment during training;
* RGB alone at inference.

The depth-regularized model reduced frozen-probe visual-odometry error by `33%` and improved out-of-domain rollout and surprise metrics. The authors’ interpretation is that SIGReg supplies diversity, while depth determines how that diversity is geometrically organized. ([arXiv][6])

This result is exceptionally close to your situation. It explicitly argues that a capacity-limited predictor can pressure the encoder to retain a representation that is simple and predictable rather than geometric—the same failure pattern you have repeatedly measured. Their response was both a geometry teacher and additional predictor capacity during training. ([arXiv][6])

The limitations are important: it is an offline representation and rollout study, not closed-loop navigation, and the depth signal makes it no longer purely RGB self-supervised during training. But it shows a plausible route to **RGB-only inference with geometry-biased training**. ([arXiv][6])

Your RQ1 prohibits privileged nonlearned map evidence **at inference**. It does not necessarily prohibit simulator depth or geometry as a training-only teacher. That boundary should be made explicit rather than assumed.

## Delta-JEPA: directly aimed at action-invariant encoder collapse

Another recent preprint, Delta-JEPA, addresses exactly the failure you observed: prediction loss can make an encoder easier to predict while making different actions less distinguishable.

It adds a latent-difference action decoder:

[
\Delta z_t=z_{t+1}-z_t
\quad\rightarrow\quad
\hat a_t.
]

The executed action must be recoverable from the change in latent state, rather than from the two endpoint states concatenated together. This directly forces actions to produce distinguishable latent displacements. Across Two-Room, Reacher, Push-T and OGBench-Cube, it reports higher planning success than PLDM, LeWorldModel and Sub-JEPA; its largest improvement over the corresponding endpoint decoder was `12.6` percentage points on Push-T. ([arXiv][7])

This is more directly aligned with your measured failure than same-state branch-ranking cross-entropy. It:

* uses every ordinary transition;
* does not require exact same-state counterfactual branches;
* constrains the encoder’s transition geometry;
* makes action invariance costly.

It remains a recent preprint evaluated on considerably simpler tasks than your Go2 problem, but it deserves explicit consideration before another hand-designed action-ranking auxiliary.

# 6. Positive quadruped JEPA evidence

*Predictive Training with Latent Imagination for Visual Quadruped Navigation* reports successful real Go2 transfer and reduced collisions from a JEPA-style auxiliary predictor. The predictor shapes the recurrent policy state during training and is discarded at inference. ([arXiv][8])

However, the policy receives:

* depth-derived visual features;
* proprioception and goal state;
* a 132-dimensional explicit obstacle representation;
* relative obstacle position, velocity, size, orientation, distance and collision threat;
* simulator ground-truth obstacle state during training, with depth detection and tracking on the robot. ([arXiv][9])

Therefore, its positive result does not demonstrate that JEPA prediction learned metric obstacle geometry from RGB. Geometry was already supplied in a highly structured form. The predictive objective improved anticipation of obstacle motion.

This is still useful evidence, but for a narrower claim:

> Predictive auxiliary training can improve quadruped navigation when the recurrent state already receives reliable geometric and dynamic inputs.

Your problem is one stage earlier: learning those inputs from RGB while preserving them through joint prediction.

# Direct answers to your questions

## Did the official base models retain spatial information?

**Yes, but at different levels.**

V-JEPA 2 retained enough coarse depth and relative object geometry for short-horizon visual servoing and manipulation. It did not initially produce especially clean dense local features. V-JEPA 2.1 produced much stronger linearly recoverable depth, boundaries and semantic structure. DINOv2 patch tokens also retained substantial local geometry, often more useful for precise planning than V-JEPA or V-JEPA 2 tokens. ([arXiv][1])

None of these results proves that the frozen features natively encode a visibility-aware egocentric occupancy map with calibrated unknown/free/occupied distinctions.

## How did they preserve it?

The strongest recurring ingredients were:

* dense patch or tubelet tokens rather than CLS/global pooling;
* high input resolution;
* masked context–target asymmetry;
* supervision of visible as well as masked tokens;
* deep supervision at intermediate encoder layers;
* large and diverse image/video pretraining;
* temporal context;
* proprioception for exact metric state;
* frozen backbones during robot dynamics training;
* short rollout losses and receding-horizon replanning. ([arXiv][2])

Recent end-to-end work additionally suggests that physically grounded auxiliary constraints—depth alignment or action reconstruction from latent displacement—may be needed to determine **how** a noncollapsed representation organizes its information. ([arXiv][6])

## Did greater training variety naturally force more geometry?

**No—not by itself.**

V-JEPA 2 had exceptional data scale and still learned fragmented local features. V-JEPA 2.1’s objective changes yielded the first large dense improvement; additional data then improved it further. Data diversity is necessary for transfer, but the objective determines whether the encoder spends capacity on local geometry, global semantics, appearance invariance or generic predictability. ([arXiv][2])

The literature also shows strong domain effects. DINOv3 helped more on photorealistic DROID and Robocasa than on simple synthetic Maze and Wall, while DINOv2 was already sufficient for the latter. More data and a larger model are not automatically better if the learned representation becomes unnecessarily complex for the available predictor or mismatched to the visual domain. ([arXiv][4])

## Did their control tasks require less geometry than yours?

**Usually less, or at least a different kind.**

The manipulation tasks required meaningful depth and object localization, so they should not be dismissed as spatially trivial. But they used fixed cameras, end-effector state, short bounded actions and frequent replanning. DINO-WM’s navigation environments were simple image-goal tasks with much more direct visual access to the state. V-JEPA 2.1 navigation was evaluated through two-second trajectory error, not false-free-space rate, collision-safe maze traversal or persistent partial observability. ([arXiv][1])

Your task requires a moving egocentric camera to support:

* reliable occupied boundaries;
* free versus unknown separation;
* action-conditioned local change;
* scene-disjoint generalization;
* collision-sensitive planning;
* persistent place memory under aliasing.

That is a stricter and more compound requirement than any one of the positive benchmarks above. Your older pooled latent failed transferable wall geometry, while the direct learned BEV variants retained genuine image-dependent structure but still failed occupied-space qualification.

# What this implies for the path forward

## 1. Bring the pretrained-representation comparison forward

Your current thesis plan places pretrained-versus-from-scratch comparison in Paper C. The literature and your failures now justify moving at least the **representation qualification portion** of that comparison into the immediate critical path. 

The most informative comparison is:

* DINOv2 or DINOv3 dense patch tokens;
* V-JEPA 2 dense tokens;
* V-JEPA 2.1 dense tokens;
* your current ViT patch tokens.

All should be assessed with the exact same scene-disjoint occupied/free/unknown probe, especially on open obstacle fields.

This is not the thesis endpoint because those encoders are frozen. It is the strongest available answer to:

> Is the geometry requirement achievable from RGB with an existing dense representation?

If V-JEPA 2.1 or DINO passes your gate, you stop guessing what geometry objective might work from scratch.

## 2. The strongest baseline should be frozen dense features plus learned dynamics

The positive embodied literature overwhelmingly uses this structure. It should be treated as the mandatory baseline:

```text
frozen dense foundation encoder
        +
three-frame context
        +
action-conditioned predictor
```

The frozen model is not your final JEPA claim. It establishes the achievable representation and prediction performance before encoder movement is introduced.

Your thesis’s stronger contribution would then be:

> Can robot-specific joint JEPA adaptation improve action-conditioned dynamics without regressing the spatial information inherited from pretraining?

That is a much cleaner question than attempting to learn geometry, semantics, dynamics and anti-collapse structure simultaneously from a modest task corpus.

## 3. For genuine encoder movement, use the V-JEPA 2.1 recipe—not unmasked all-token regression

The literature supports:

* three-frame or longer temporal context;
* masked spatiotemporal context;
* a distinct future target position;
* dense loss on visible and masked tokens;
* deep supervision of intermediate encoder layers;
* target normalization and slow EMA;
* dense patch tokens as the operational predictive state.

Your recent single-frame all-token MSE experiments offered the encoder an easy route toward generic predictability. V-JEPA 2.1’s context–target asymmetry is specifically intended to prevent visible tokens from becoming weakly localized global aggregators.

So the proposed masked three-frame direction is well supported—but it should incorporate **dense context supervision and deep self-supervision**, not merely mask some tokens and otherwise reproduce the existing objective.

## 4. Separate the geometry and action-sensitivity interventions

The recent literature suggests two distinct failure corrections:

* **Geometry failure:** training-only depth alignment or another physically grounded dense target.
* **Action-invariance failure:** decode action from the latent displacement, Delta-JEPA style.

Do not add both in the first successor. First determine whether a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 or DINO representation already passes the geometry gate.

* If it does, the next problem is controlled joint adaptation and action sensitivity.
* If it does not, add a geometry-oriented training signal before worrying about rollout.
* If geometry survives but action margin again falls during encoder movement, latent-difference action decoding is a better-supported intervention than another same-state ranking loss.

## 5. Reconsider the `112×112` input before concluding that isolated obstacles are unlearnable

V-JEPA 2.1’s depth performance benefited substantially from high-resolution cooldown, and all strong dense foundation encoders operate natively around `224–384` pixels. Your `112×112` model has a `16×16` token grid, but each token originates from far fewer source pixels.

That may be adequate for large maze walls and inadequate for isolated or distant obstacles—exactly the family pattern you observed. The next dense-backbone screen should use the backbone’s native resolution rather than first crushing every input to `112×112`.

## 6. Retain your stricter spatial qualification

The literature’s success criteria do not make your occupied-space gate excessive. They make it valuable.

Depth and segmentation probes show recoverability. Robot success shows integrated utility. Neither proves that a planner will not mistake an obstacle for free space. Your requirement to qualify occupied precision/recall, visibility, scene-family transfer and open-field performance is a defensible thesis contribution rather than merely an implementation burden.

# Overall assessment

Your recent failures do **not** suggest that JEPA-based embodied control is implausible. They show that you have been attempting a particularly difficult combination that most positive papers avoid:

> a compact, task-trained, moving encoder that must learn RGB geometry and action dynamics simultaneously, while satisfying strict scene-disjoint occupied-space criteria.

The successful literature usually takes one of two easier routes:

1. inherit rich dense geometry from internet-scale pretraining and freeze the encoder; or
2. jointly train a compact model on much simpler, more fully observed control tasks, often with proprioception or explicit geometric supervision.

The clearest literature-informed path is therefore:

> **First qualify frozen V-JEPA 2.1 and DINO dense tokens on your exact spatial gate. Then use the strongest representation as the initialization and baseline for a masked, three-frame, dense token JEPA with controlled top-block encoder movement. Add a training-only geometry teacher only if pretrained tokens still fail geometry; add latent-displacement action decoding only if joint adaptation again erodes action sensitivity.**

That sequence directly tests what the positive literature has established, while preserving the harder and genuinely distinctive contribution of your thesis.
